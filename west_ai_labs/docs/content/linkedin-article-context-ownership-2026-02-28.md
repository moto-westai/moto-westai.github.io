# Whoever Owns the Context, Owns the Relationship

*LinkedIn Article — updated 2026-02-28*
*Author: Jason West, West AI Labs*

---

Anthropic just made it easier to import your ChatGPT memories into Claude.

The top comment on their announcement: *"I exported all my memories last night, canceled my ChatGPT sub, and deleted all my data."*

People are celebrating this as data portability. I see it differently.

You didn't get your memories back. You just moved them to a different landlord.

---

Here's what "memory" actually means in an AI context: it's everything the system knows about you. Your preferences. Your projects. Your communication style. Your business context. The shorthand you've developed over hundreds of conversations.

That's not just data. That's **relationship capital** — the accumulated understanding that makes an AI assistant actually useful instead of generic.

And right now, that relationship capital lives on someone else's servers, under someone else's terms, subject to someone else's pricing decisions, deprecation schedules, and legal obligations.

You can export it today. Great. What happens when the import format changes? When the memory limit drops from 25,000 characters to 10,000? When your vendor gets acquired, subpoenaed, or simply decides your use case isn't profitable enough to support?

The comment that should have gotten more attention wasn't the celebratory one. It was this:

**"Whoever owns the context, owns the relationship."**

---

We've been through this before. We learned it with email (remember when your contacts lived in Outlook PST files on a company server?). We learned it with social media (your followers on someone else's platform aren't really yours). We're learning it again with cloud infrastructure.

Every time, the pattern is the same: convenience now, lock-in later, extraction eventually.

AI memory is just the next version of this story — and it's moving faster because the stakes are higher. Your AI assistant doesn't just know your email habits. It knows your business strategy, your customer relationships, your decision-making patterns. That's the most sensitive context you've ever handed to a vendor.

---

I'm not saying don't use Claude or ChatGPT. Use them. They're extraordinary tools.

I'm saying: be clear-eyed about what you're handing over and to whom.

The engineers and founders I talk to who are most sophisticated about this aren't the ones avoiding AI. They're the ones asking a different question: *what would it look like if we owned this layer ourselves?*

Local inference is no longer a science project. Apple Silicon changed the economics. A Mac Mini M4 Pro can run capable models at full speed with 48GB of unified memory. A single machine can serve a small team, keep all context local, and cost less per year than a mid-tier SaaS subscription.

The context — the memory, the relationship, the accumulated intelligence — stays in your building. On your hardware. Under your control.

---

The AI race right now is being framed as model quality. GPT-5 vs. Claude 4 vs. Gemini Ultra. Who has the smartest model.

I think that's the wrong race to watch.

The real competition is for context ownership. Who accumulates the deepest understanding of your business, your team, your workflows. Who has the most irreplaceable relationship with you.

Right now, the answer is: whoever you've been talking to.

Make sure that's someone you can trust with everything — because that's what you're giving them.

---

**This week made that point impossible to ignore.**

The Trump administration just blacklisted Anthropic from Pentagon contracts — invoking national security law after Anthropic refused to allow their technology to be used for mass surveillance of U.S. citizens or for autonomous weapons systems that could select and kill targets without human input.

Let that sink in.

One of the world's most capable AI companies is now barred from federal work because they drew a line: *a human must remain in the loop before AI takes a life.* And they're being punished for it.

Meanwhile, MIT physicist Max Tegmark — who has spent a decade warning about ungoverned AI — put it plainly: we have more regulation on sandwich shops than on AI systems. Because the AI companies themselves lobbied against binding oversight for years, promising to self-regulate. And one by one, they've broken those promises. Google dropped "Don't be evil." OpenAI removed safety from their mission statement. Now we have no referee, no framework, no floor.

What happens when the vendor your business depends on gets banned by executive order? When their ethics don't align with whoever holds power? When the AI that knows your company's most sensitive context is operating under someone else's values?

---

This is why I keep coming back to a question that doesn't get asked enough:

**It's not just *how* you train your AI. It's *who* trains it.**

The values baked into a model — what it refuses to do, what it defaults to, how it handles ambiguity — come from somewhere. They come from the people who built it, the incentives they operate under, and the masters they ultimately serve.

An AI that can surveil citizens without consent, or make lethal decisions without a human in the loop, isn't a safety failure. It's a values failure. And values failures at AI scale aren't hypothetical anymore. They're this week's news.

I'm not a doomer. I think we're living through one of the most extraordinary moments in human history. Genuinely exciting. But "exciting" and "dangerous" aren't opposites — they're the same thing viewed from different angles.

The builders who take this seriously aren't the ones shutting AI down. They're the ones insisting on guardrails, human oversight, and infrastructure they actually control.

Local-first isn't just a technical preference. It's a values statement.
